First up, Walmart is setting the record straight about its new digital shelf labels. As the retail giant rolls out these electronic screens to thousands of stores, some shoppers worried that artificial intelligence, or A-I, would be used to raise prices on the spot. But Walmart's chief executive says that is simply not the case. The company insists it does not use personal information to set prices, nor do they plan on personalized pricing. The digital tags are just to help employees update prices quickly and keep shelves stocked.

Next, real estate mogul Grant Cardone is warning of a historic crash in the commercial real estate market. Cardone believes high interest rates are squeezing property owners, which could lead to massive defaults. Interestingly, he is pointing to Bitcoin as a complementary asset rather than a rival. He suggests that while traditional real estate provides steady cash flow, digital assets like Bitcoin can offer a modern hedge against inflation. It is a striking shift from a traditional hard-asset investor.

Speaking of digital assets, decentralized finance, often called dee-fy, is looking for ways to go mainstream. Kash Dhanda from Jupiter Exchange recently shared how the industry can bridge the gap to everyday users. He says the key is simplifying the user experience. Right now, decentralized finance can feel too complex for the average person. By focusing on intuitive design and security, the industry hopes to transition from a tech-heavy niche into a welcoming financial tool for everyone.

In health and technology, British politician Louise Haigh has hinted at a potential ban on the American software firm Palantir from working with the National Health Service, or N-H-S. Palantir, a massive data analytics company, has faced intense scrutiny over how it handles sensitive patient data. This political pushback highlights the growing global debate over whether private tech giants should be trusted with public health records, especially when privacy and corporate profits collide.

Startup companies like Anduril are challenging traditional defense giants to build advanced, self-flying drones for the United States Air Force. These autonomous aircraft are designed to fly alongside human pilots in combat. This shift toward private tech startups is transforming the defense sector, bringing faster software development and A-I straight to the front lines of modern aviation.
